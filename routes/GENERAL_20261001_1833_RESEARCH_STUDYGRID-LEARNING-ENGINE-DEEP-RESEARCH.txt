CORE RESEARCH QUESTION
What should StudyGrid own as a proprietary learning engine, what should it borrow from proven research/open-source systems/standards, and what evidence should drive its next-action decisions?

RESEARCH SCOPE
Build an evidence-grounded map of the problem before recommending an engine design. Cover at minimum:

A. LEARNER STATE / KNOWLEDGE ESTIMATION
- Bayesian Knowledge Tracing and interpretable variants
- Performance Factors Analysis / learning-curve approaches
- Item Response Theory and multidimensional/diagnostic variants where relevant
- Cognitive Diagnosis Models / skill mastery models
- Deep Knowledge Tracing / transformer-style approaches and their evidence, data requirements, calibration and interpretability trade-offs
- uncertainty/calibration: how sure can the system reasonably be that a learner knows something?

B. MEMORY / RETRIEVAL / FORGETTING
- spacing effect, retrieval practice, interleaving where evidence supports it
- modern spaced-repetition schedulers such as FSRS and established alternatives
- how memory models should interact with course prerequisites and exam urgency rather than acting as a standalone flashcard scheduler

C. KNOWLEDGE / CURRICULUM STRUCTURE
- concept/skill graphs, prerequisite relations, curriculum sequencing, knowledge spaces, mastery learning
- what should be authored, inferred, or learned from data
- cold-start strategies when the course is new and little learner data exists

D. NEXT-ACTION / ADAPTIVE POLICY
Research how systems choose among TEACH / EXPLAIN / EXAMPLE / RETRIEVE / PRACTISE / REVIEW / REMEDIATE / ADVANCE / ASSESS / STOP/PAUSE.
Compare rule-based policies, mastery thresholds, bandit/recommender approaches, reinforcement-learning approaches and hybrid systems. Pay special attention to causal validity: prediction accuracy alone does not prove a recommendation improves learning.

E. ASSESSMENT EVIDENCE
- interaction types and construct alignment
- question/item difficulty, discrimination and partial credit where appropriate
- error/misconception patterns
- response time and why it can be misleading
- confidence/metacognitive judgements and evidence on when they add useful signal
- hints, retries, answer changes, explanation requests and other process data
- distinguishing exposure/completion from actual learning

F. TEACHING / FEEDBACK
- worked examples, fading/scaffolding, feedback timing/content, example→practice progression, generation effects where relevant
- when to teach before testing versus test first
- how an engine can choose a teaching intervention rather than only choosing a question

G. INSTRUCTOR / CLASS-LEVEL EVIDENCE
Research what aggregated signals can responsibly support instructor decisions: misconceptions, prerequisite bottlenecks, weak concepts, content that appears ineffective, cohort trends, uncertainty, and intervention suggestions. Distinguish descriptive evidence from unsupported causal claims. Include research on learning analytics dashboards and what actually helps instructors versus adding noise.

H. OPEN-SOURCE / EXISTING-SYSTEM STUDY
Study mature systems/libraries/repos and standards where relevant. Include at least some of: ALEKS/Knowledge Space Theory literature; ASSISTments; Open edX adaptive/assessment architecture; Moodle/adaptive plugins if genuinely relevant; pyBKT or comparable knowledge-tracing implementations; FSRS/open-spaced-repetition; QTI; CASE; Caliper/xAPI if useful; open-source recommender/educational-data-mining implementations. For every codebase/library studied, report:
- what problem it solves well;
- architectural choice worth learning from;
- testing/validation approach;
- known limitations or ugly seams;
- licence/IP implications;
- whether StudyGrid should adopt, adapt, wrap, learn from only, or avoid.
Do not treat popularity as proof of pedagogical effectiveness.

I. DATA / PRIVACY / INSTITUTIONAL READINESS
- minimum useful data for Engine v1
- signals that are tempting but noisy/invasive
- data provenance and model/version provenance
- separation of individual and cohort data
- explainability/auditability
- privacy/fairness risks, institutional trust, student agency and instructor controls
- what data should never be required simply because it could improve prediction

J. FAILURE MODES / RED TEAM
Find ways adaptive-learning engines fail: cold start, sparse data, overconfidence, mis-specified skill maps, content leakage, gaming, answer memorization, rapid guessing, confounding instruction with ability, poor calibration, cohort/domain shift, over-personalization, local optimum/trapping a learner, accessibility mismatch, bad source material, AI-generated content errors, and feedback loops that reinforce bad assumptions.

K. BETA / EXPERIMENTATION
Nathan expects real beta access soon. Recommend the smallest instrumentation and experiments that could falsify assumptions safely with ~20 students + one professor. Avoid pretending such a cohort proves long-term efficacy. Identify what can be learned from usability, behaviour, calibration and qualitative interviews versus what requires a larger/longer study.
